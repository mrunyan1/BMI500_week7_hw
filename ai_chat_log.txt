❯ what is the repoducitnp ate

⏺ The basic reproduction number, R₀, is the average number of people one
  infected person passes the disease to when everyone else is still susceptible
  (the very start of an outbreak).

  For the SIR model in your homework:

  R₀ = βN / γ

  The reason it has that form: one infected person infects others at rate βS per
  day, which at the start is about βN. They stay infectious for an average of
  1/γ days. Multiply the two: (βN) × (1/γ) = βN/γ infections per infected
  person.

  For your Part B numbers: 0.000125 × 2000 / 0.1 = 2.5, so each case leads to
  about 2.5 new cases early on.

  Why it matters:
  - R₀ > 1: each person infects more than one other, so cases grow and you get
    an epidemic.
  - R₀ < 1: each person infects fewer than one other, so the outbreak dies out.
  - R₀ = 1 is the dividing line, which is why Part E asks you to draw that line
    on the heatmap.

  In the lecture's notation (slide 35) it's written R₀ = α/β, since Sameni uses
  α for transmission and β for recovery. Same quantity, different letters.

  The Part D version, R₀ = βNσ/((σ+μ)(γ+μ)), is the same idea adjusted for the
  fact that an exposed person might die (μ) before becoming infectious (σ), and
  an infectious person leaves the pool by recovery or death (γ+μ).

❯ but in B 5 already are infected

⏺ Right, and that's why the formula uses N as an approximation. The exact
  starting number would be βS(0)/γ = 0.000125 × 1995 / 0.1 = 2.49, since each of
  those 5 infected people can only infect the 1995 who are still susceptible,
  not all 2000.

  The difference between 2.49 and 2.5 is tiny because 5 out of 2000 is
  negligible. R₀ is defined as a property of the disease in a fully susceptible
  population, which is why the handout writes it with N: it's the "what if
  everyone were susceptible" number, not the number at any specific moment.

  The quantity that does change as the epidemic runs is the effective
  reproduction number, βS(t)/γ. It starts at about 2.5 and falls as S drops.
  When S reaches 800, it equals exactly 1, and that's your peak: at that point
  each infected person is replacing themselves with one new case, so I stops
  growing. That's the same S = γ/β condition you derived in Part B, just viewed
  from a different angle.

  For your hypothesis page, R₀ = 2.5 is fine. If you want to be precise you can
  write "R₀ = βN/γ = 2.5 (2.49 using S(0) = 1995)."

❯ why we know at the peak that Infefciton rate is not changeing becuase peak is
  flat

⏺ Yes, that's exactly it. A peak is the top of a hill: just before it the curve
  is rising, just after it the curve is falling, and at the very top it's flat
  for an instant. A flat curve means the slope is zero, and the slope of I(t) is
  dI/dt. So at the peak, dI/dt = 0.

  That's the whole reason you can write βSI − γI = 0 and solve for S. You're
  saying "find the moment when I stops rising," and the equation tells you that
  happens when S has dropped to γ/β = 800.

  One small wording point: it's the number infected that isn't changing at the
  peak, not the "infection rate." New infections are still happening (βSI = 0.1
  × I per day) and recoveries are still happening (γI, also 0.1 × I per day).
  They're just equal, so the inflow and outflow cancel and I holds still for
  that instant. On your page you already have it right: "infection inflow βSI
  equals the recovery outflow γI."

  If you want to say it in one line: at the peak I is momentarily flat, so dI/dt
  = 0, which means new infections and recoveries are balanced.

❯ but then ODe for I we set change of I to 0 then we plug in S and solve for I?

⏺ No, and this is the key thing to see. Try it:

  βSI − γI = 0
  I(βS − γ) = 0

  That means either I = 0 or βS − γ = 0. The I = 0 solution just says "no
  infected people" (the disease is gone), which isn't the peak. The other
  solution, S = γ/β = 800, is the peak, but notice I has vanished from it.
  Because every term in dI/dt is multiplied by I, the equation factors it out,
  and I itself doesn't appear in the answer.

  So setting dI/dt = 0 tells you when the peak happens (S = 800) but can never
  tell you how tall it is. Any value of I makes the equation true at S = 800,
  which is why that equation alone can't give you a number.

  To get I at the peak you need a second piece of information. The one on your
  page is the simplest: by the peak, S has dropped from 1995 to 800, so 1195
  people have moved out of S into either I or R. The people in R are the ones
  who already recovered, so I at the peak = 1195 minus however many have
  recovered by then, plus the 5 you started with. That's where the "maybe a
  third to a half have recovered" guess comes in, and it's a guess, which is
  fine: that's what a hypothesis is.

❯ From the ODE, dI/dt = (βS − γ)I, so at time 0 the infected pool grows at a
  rate of (0.000125 × 1995 − 0.1) ≈ 0.149 per day, --here why not multiply by I
  whcih is 5 to start

⏺ Both are correct; they're two different rates.

  - (βS − γ) × I = 0.149 × 5 ≈ 0.75 people per day. That's the absolute rate:
    how many people are added to I on day 0. Your original draft had this number
    (0.746875).
  - (βS − γ) = 0.149 per day. That's the per-person rate: each infected person
    adds 0.149 new net infections per day, or put another way, I grows by about
    16% per day.

  The second one is more useful here because the absolute rate changes every
  day. On day 0 it's 0.75 people/day, but once I has grown to 50 it's 0.149 × 50
  ≈ 7.5 people/day, and at 500 it's about 75/day. That's exponential growth:
  the more infected people there are, the faster it grows. The per-person rate
  0.149 stays roughly constant while S ≈ 1995, which is why you can use it to
  estimate how long the climb takes: a 16%-per-day growth needs about
  ln(100)/0.149 ≈ 31 days to multiply by 100.

  The 0.75 number is actually why your original draft's estimate came out at 956
  days: it assumed the absolute rate stays at 0.75 forever, when really it
  keeps increasing. You spotted that yourself when you called it a drastic
  overestimate.

  If you want the page to show both, you could write: "at time 0 the net rate is
  (βS − γ)I ≈ 0.149 × 5 ≈ 0.75 people/day, but the per-person growth rate 0.149
  per day is what matters because I grows exponentially." That's a nice way to
  flag that you understand the difference.

❯ 16 percent or 0.149?

⏺ Same thing, two ways of saying it. 0.149 is the rate; 16% is what it works out
  to per day.

  The ODE dI/dt = 0.149 × I says I grows continuously at rate 0.149. Over one
  full day of continuous growth, I is multiplied by e^0.149 ≈ 1.16, so the
  day-over-day increase is about 16%. (If you just added 0.149 once per day
  you'd get 14.9%, but the growth compounds within the day, which bumps it to
  16%.)

  For the page, keep it simple: write 0.149 per day where you have the formula,
  since that's what comes straight from βS − γ, and say "roughly 16% per day"
  once after it so the doubling argument makes sense. Something like:

  ▎ …grows at a rate of (0.000125 × 1995 − 0.1) ≈ 0.149 per day, meaning I is
  ▎ multiplied by about 1.16 each day (roughly 16% growth).

  Then the "1.16 multiplied by itself about 4.5 times ≈ 2" line follows
  naturally. If someone asks where 1.16 comes from, it's e^0.149.

❯ what detmines when person goes from recovered ot succsoetile in sir model

⏺ Nothing. In the plain SIR model, recovered people never return to susceptible.
  The arrows go one way, S → I → R, and the equations show it: dR/dt = γI only
  adds to R, and nothing in dS/dt = −βSI adds to S. Once someone recovers,
  they're immune for good.

  That's also why the SIR epidemic always dies out on its own: S only ever goes
  down, so eventually it drops below γ/β and the infection can't sustain itself.

  Where you have seen a return arrow is Sameni's slide 31, "an epidemic without
  life-time immunity." That's the SIRS variant: there's an extra term, +γR in
  dS/dt and −γR in dR/dt (his γ there is the immunity-loss rate, not the
  recovery rate), and immunity wears off at a constant rate. That's what
  produces the repeated waves on slide 35.

  Your HW1 Part D doesn't have that either. The SEIR model there keeps lifetime
  immunity. What refills S is births (+μN), and deaths remove people from every
  compartment (−μS, −μE, −μI, −μR). So in your homework the waves come from new
  susceptibles being born, not from recovered people losing immunity. That's the
  distinction you wrote on your hypothesis page.
